The Power of Free

How free communication in prisons and jails is transforming lives, strengthening families, and reshaping reentry. A necessary, achievable, and transformative lifeline.

About the Report

FREE COMMUNICATION Changes Everything

Correctional telecom is a $1.5 billion industry built on charging incarcerated people and their families exorbitant rates to stay connected. When families can't afford to call, communication gets rationed — conversations cut short, contact limited to urgent matters, relationships fracture or never form. That isolation doesn't stay inside the walls. It weakens the ties that matter most to rehabilitation, reentry, and public safety.

The national Connecting Families campaign is making communication in prisons and jails entirely free across the country. This report is the first to document the results — and the evidence is clear: when the cost barrier is removed, connection increases, relationships strengthen, and families are better able to navigate incarceration together toward a promising free future that makes us all safer.

Key Findings

8 Areas of Measurable Impact

Drawing on data and interviews across multiple jurisdictions, eight areas of meaningful, measurable change emerged — for incarcerated people, their families, corrections staff, and the broader public.

Finding 1

INCREASED Connection

Today, over 330,000 incarcerated people have access to fully free phone calls, and in some cases video calls and electronic messaging too, thanks to free communication policies in their jurisdictions. When the cost barrier to connection was removed, communication increased immediately, dramatically, and durably.

These policies have enabled nearly 600 million additional calls and 6.4 billion additional call minutes between incarcerated people and their loved ones — that's millions more "I love yous" and billions more minutes of laughter, love, and hope.

Love without limits

What had once been rationed and rushed became abundant and consistent. In prisons, average daily usage increased from 25 to 45 minutes per person. In jails, the jump was even steeper — from 27 to 57 minutes, reflecting the urgent needs of people recently incarcerated and those awaiting trial.

But this isn't just about quantity, it's also about quality. Without the constant stress of cost, conversations moved from brief, transactional agendas to sustained, relational connection. People were thoughtfully checking in on one another, doing homework with their children, watching movies with their spouses, and just being present for life's ups and downs — all without a clock running. The minutes matter, but what happens inside them matters more.

▶ More "I love yous"

"We have seen a significant increase in call activity, demonstrating that when financial barriers are removed, individuals in our care make greater use of opportunities to stay connected."

— Commissioner Dan Martuscello III, New York

Finding 2

FINANCIAL Relief

For decades, correctional telecom providers have charged incarcerated people and their families exorbitant rates to stay in touch. Given the remarkably low wages, if any, paid to incarcerated people, it is often families on the outside who bear this cost — households with limited financial means already destabilized by the incarceration of a family member. One in three families goes into debt trying to stay connected, and 87% of those carrying the financial burden are women.

The financial burden also carries immense emotional weight. Incarcerated people described a persistent sense of embarrassment and shame in asking family to pay for calls. When free communication policies were implemented, a weight was lifted on both sides of the wall.

$622 MIL

Groceries, rent, and room to breathe

Savings for families with loved ones in prison ranged from $172 to $1,801 per year, depending on previous per-minute rates. They also accrued substantial value from the increase in free call time, which ranged from $244 to $2,927 per year. An outsized share of those savings flowed to the communities disproportionately impacted by incarceration — 70% of prison savings and 82% of jail savings went to Black and brown families. That new financial capacity did not sit idle — families used it to cover important expenses, reduce debt, and invest in their futures.

Notably, the cost of communication also fell dramatically when the cost burden was shifted off of families to agencies — a direct reflection of the perverse incentives and overcharging that have dominated the industry.

▶ A weight's been lifted

"We have been able to take that money and put it into a down payment for a mortgage. We've already saved $10,000 to put towards our future life together."

— Brashani, Wife of an incarcerated man, Massachusetts

Finding 3

STRONGER FAMILY & COMMUNITY Relationships

Before the shift to free communication, incarcerated people and their families would go days, weeks, months, and even years between calls. Conversations were rationed around cost and limited to the most urgent matters. Communication was constrained and transactional, often focused on logistics or immediate needs rather than meaningful connection. But when the cost of communication was removed, that dynamic shifted.

Share of people who said free calls helped build or repair relationships — the most common theme identified in interviews

82%

family members

Support, both ways

Without the financial stressor of paying for calls, communication centered on strengthening or repairing relationships. Incarcerated people and their loved ones expressed needing each other, rooting relationships in reciprocity that ensured support and accountability flowed both ways. And in doing so, this connection created a renewed sense of belonging and hope for the future. With more time and fewer constraints, families returned to the roles and rhythms of everyday life.

These connections don't just sustain people through incarceration. Maintaining, and even expanding, social ties is critical for incarcerated people and their successful rehabilitation and reentry. They preserve the relationships that make hope, healing, and rehabilitation possible — and they become the foundation on which successful reentry is built.

▶ "Mutual support"

"I didn't feel like I'm looking at the time [and need] to hurry up and get through everything that I want to share within a 20-minute phone call. They can speak, and I can actually hear my family, loved ones, or friends vent about issues that they're having. I could literally just listen to them."

— Hamza, Formerly incarcerated, Massachusetts

Finding 4

PARENTING & CHILD Development

Nearly half of people in prison are parents to minor children, with 18% of those children four years old or younger. For incarcerated parents, connecting with their children over the phone is one of the primary ways they parent while separated. But the high cost of calls has long made that connection difficult to sustain at the levels necessary for child rearing. Parents are often forced to consider whether to spend their families' limited resources on staying involved in their children's lives or on other needs for their care and upbringing. The consistency that builds trust is the first to go.

"You can't force a child to say what's going on in 30 minutes. But you can hear if your child is going through something by the tone of their voice. With free phone calls, if she has something going on, she can talk to me."

— Natavia, Currently Incarcerated, New York

For the next generation

Free communication restored that consistency. Parents describe children running to the phone, eagerly anticipating daily calls, opening up in ways that intermittent, rationed conversations never allowed. For one family, daily calls between a father and his autistic daughter produced a concrete developmental change: she started speaking more, becoming vocal in ways she had never been before.

Stronger parent-child relationships can help mitigate the harm of parental incarceration and improve outcomes for millions of impacted children — and the generations that follow. By enabling consistent parental engagement and supporting child development, free communication policies strengthen family stability and generational mobility.

▶ "I raised my daughter over the phone"

"When I say I raised my daughter over the phone, I mean it in the literal sense. Ninety-five percent of our engagement was over the phone — reading books to her, helping her with her homework, and praying with her."

— Michael, Formerly Incarcerated, New York

Finding 5

IMPROVED MENTAL & PHYSICAL Health

Prisons and jails are isolating by design. Incarceration cuts people off from their families and communities, leaving many feeling abandoned, worthless, and hopeless. These emotions erode the mental health of incarcerated people as well as their loved ones, who suffer real loss when they are removed from the home. Phone calls are a literal lifeline — and when they became free, people used them.

Described improvements in stress, emotional stability, or overall wellbeing once calls became free

77%

family members

76%

incarcerated people

Hope is on the line

With free communication, families were able to stay present in one another's lives, share emotional support, and move through daily challenges together rather than apart. Calls eased loneliness and depression for incarcerated people, bringing back the sense of belonging incarceration often strips away and restoring the hope needed to keep going. These individual improvements in mental health rippled outward — a calmer person is a safer person. And for their loved ones on the outside, that same connection relieved worry and mitigated loss.

Access to free communication also helped incarcerated people get urgent medical attention. Unable to call 911 or access emergency medical help, incarcerated people must rely on correctional officers to receive medical attention. When such efforts prove unsuccessful, incarcerated people could communicate urgent health needs to loved ones who could advocate for them by calling the facility and escalating their medical concerns.

▶ "Connection as medicine"

"Without the free phone calls, all your connections are cut off. You are literally isolated from everything. You get a sense of hope when you can still interact with people on the outside and see there's people out there that care about you."

— Robert, Formerly Incarcerated, California

Finding 6

LESS VIOLENCE & Operational Disruption

Phones have long been one of the most volatile pressure points inside correctional facilities — where incarcerated people get good news and bad news that can change their day or their life. Artificial scarcity created by high call costs, limited infrastructure, and restrictive access meant that phones were a consistent source of tension. Ultimately, emotionally charged conversations were often cut short, leaving people to carry unresolved stress back into shared living spaces, which expectedly created broader environmental issues.

100%

of corrections staff recommended free communication as a security tool that reduced tensions and makes their jobs easier.

Calmer and safer facilities

Free communication removed the scarcity that drove conflict, especially when implemented over personal tablets. Incarcerated people no longer had to compete for limited phone time or worry about when they would next be able to speak with loved ones. The prison environment had less tension, fewer conflicts, and more emotional stability. Overall, facilities became calmer, making both incarcerated people and corrections staff safer.

With fewer conflicts and increased support from loved ones in navigating emotionally charged situations, there was a noticeable decrease in disciplinary infractions. These infractions often carry serious consequences that can affect a person's eligibility for early release, including parole. Thus, this shift not only improved daily life within facilities but also potentially enabled some incarcerated people to go home sooner.

▶ "If they're having a good day, we're having a good day"

"I would go for it. It's going to be an enhancement for the incarcerated population and an enhancement for the security and civilian staff. It's a win-win on both sides."

— Sergeant Mitchell, New York

Finding 7

REHABILITATION

For many incarcerated people, staying connected to loved ones is not just about maintaining relationships — it is a catalyst for personal growth. Staying in touch allows people to remain connected to who they are as parents, partners, and members of their communities, rather than becoming defined solely by their time inside. This sense of identity and responsibility is motivating — when people feel connected to those who care about them and can see fruitful lives post incarceration, they are more likely to invest in their own growth and wellbeing.

"I see different individuals [when] they connect with their sons or their daughters or their wife. They're exercising more, getting involved in groups, going to church. Why? They want to become better men because they're making these connections. I can see their attitudes — their moves — have changed. And I can see life, I can see the energy, I can see excitement. I can see the desire to do something different."

— Saul, Currently Incarcerated, California

Staying on track

Regular communication with loved ones strengthens rehabilitation efforts by helping incarcerated people stay grounded in relationships that encourage self-improvement, accountability, and stability. When access is consistent, these connections become an ongoing source of motivation to participate in rehabilitative programs and avoid disciplinary infractions, both of which also influence early release decisions.

In helping incarcerated people maintain identity, connection, and a sense of responsibility to others, free communication supports rehabilitation not by replacing formal programs, but by strengthening the social foundations that make those programs effective. In this way, these connections are not separate from rehabilitation, but part of it, perhaps even the root of it.

▶ "Hope has a phone number"

"Conversations create a pathway for empathy and healing — and actually, just hope for a future when I get out. Without these phone calls, that would not have been possible. They were vital for my healing, for my sobriety, for my growth, and for my ability to do the positive things I'm doing now in prison — because I have a belief that there are people out there who care for me, who love me, and who I am responsible for."

— Jasmeel, Currently Incarcerated, California

Finding 8

REENTRY PLANNING & Success

Reentry can be daunting. Many incarcerated people are released without housing, employment, or the community support they need to help them navigate freedom — feeding high recidivism rates that undermine public safety. But research has repeatedly shown that having strong and consistent community ties is among the most important factors in reentry success. These ties increase the likelihood of securing housing, landing employment, and finding stability. Ultimately, the same connections that motivate rehabilitation make reentry possible — they become the foundation on which successful return is built.

"It was because of free phone calls that I was able to call the organization that ended up connecting me with the university. Within a month I'm being hired in this amazing job!"

— Elizabeth, Formerly Incarcerated, California

Safer communities

Free communication allows incarcerated people to plan for life after release while still inside, coordinating housing, employment, education, and treatment with the support of loved ones before the prison door opens. The bonds built through consistent connection during incarceration become the social infrastructure that makes reintegration possible.

Access to free communication did not just give incarcerated people the means to plan for their freedom, but also to fight for it — participating in their own defense, preparing for parole hearings, or creating required release plans. For some, it meant coming home sooner.

Successful reentry is not just a personal outcome — it is a public one. When people return home with stable housing, employment, and strong support networks, communities are safer and public systems are less burdened. Free communication is an investment in our communities that reaps returns.

▶ "The calls that come before freedom"

"I was able to call social services, call transitional housing, call the school, call the Rock program. I got out in December, and in January, I was in school. All of that was possible because we didn't have to pay for the phone."

— Donald, Formerly Incarcerated, California

ABOUT THIS REPORT

The Power of Free Communication in Prisons and Jails is a project of Worth Rises, a non-profit dedicated to dismantling the prison industry and ending the exploitation of those it touches. Since 2018, Worth Rises has led the national Connecting Families campaign aimed at making communication in prisons and jails free.

This report was made possible by the contributions of directly impacted communities — the incarcerated and formerly incarcerated people and their family members whose experiences with correctional communication are centered, and the correctional staff who offered their perspectives on how free communication policies shape facilities. We thank them for their participation.

Worth Rises is also grateful to the hundreds of national, state, and local partners, policymakers, and donors whose collaboration and support made this report — and the campaigns behind it — possible. We also thank the many people who contributed research, writing, editing, and design at every stage of this project.
